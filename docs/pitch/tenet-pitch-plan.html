02

The problem statement

0:08–0:33 · 25 sPitching

On the slide

  • The full problem statement as a quote, then the four keywords defined in general terms, before any radiography. Key words are in bold, as in your example slide.
  • Slide 5 maps the same four keywords onto radiography, so the audience sees the general idea first and the specific one second.

“The challenge: let safety personnel keep completing security processes offline, and sync correctly when the internet returns. Safety personnel protect workers and the public from hazards. Security processes are the repeatable steps that protect assets. Offline means no internet, though nearby phones can still talk to each other. And syncing correctly means every phone’s changes merge into one record, with nothing lost or doubled.”

~65 words

Offline, defined: your example slide said “communication within the same subnet”. Tenet’s phones talk by tap and QR code with no network at all, so “nearby devices can still talk directly” fits the app better.

03

Why radiography?

0:33–0:53 · 20 sPitchingOriginality

On the slide

  • A weld cross-section: the source above, steel plates joined by a weld with a hidden crack, and the film below showing it.
  • Three reasons it’s used: it’s non-destructive, welds need proof before going into service, and a gamma source goes where X-ray machines can’t.
  • One line on the stakes: a failed weld means a leak, a fire or an explosion.

“Our setting: industrial radiography. Every ship hull, pipeline and pressure tank is held together by welds, and a hidden crack can mean a leak or an explosion. Radiography X-rays a weld without cutting it open. A gamma source needs no power and fits inside a tank, so it’s a standard way to prove a weld is sound.”

~55 words

Why it matters to the pitch: judges need to know the job is valuable before they care that it’s dangerous. This slide makes the risk on the next one feel worth managing.

04

How it’s done, and the danger

0:53–1:13 · 20 sPitchingOriginality

On the slide

  • A simple diagram of the job: the shielded camera, the guide tube, the iridium-192 source wound out to the weld, the film behind it, and the cordon around it.
  • Three facts: what it is, where it’s done in Singapore, and why it’s dangerous (IAEA Category 2, and a security risk if stolen).
  • One line tying it to the brief: the IAEA’s rulebook is the Code of Conduct on the Safety and Security of Radioactive Sources, the brief’s two words.

“The radiographer winds a sealed iridium-192 source out of a shielded camera to the weld, with film behind it and a cordon around it. Singapore’s shipyards and refineries do this every day. But the IAEA rates these sources Category 2: minutes to hours of handling can permanently injure. And if one is stolen, it’s a dirty-bomb risk.”

~55 words

Checked: industrial gamma radiography sources are IAEA Category 2, which can permanently injure someone who handles them for minutes to hours (regulator summary of the IAEA categories, NRC event report). For the exact wording, check IAEA RS-G-1.9 before quoting it on stage.

The gap

1:55–2:10 · 15 sPitching

09

How a handover works

2:18–2:45 · 27 sFunctionalityOriginality

On the slide

  • One handover from left to right: the giver signs, the offer goes over a tap or QR, the receiver’s checks turn green, the seal is scanned, and the receiver countersigns.
  • Three cards for what makes it stand out: the hardware key, signed-not-secret offers, and the double handover refused on the spot.

“Ravi signs with his fingerprint, using a key locked in the phone’s secure chip. The offer goes over a tap or a QR code: made out to Siti, usable once, gone in 60 seconds. Siti’s phone checks it offline, she scans the seal and countersigns. Try to hand the same camera over twice, and the phone refuses.”

~55 words · the demo proves each card
10

Sync: one checked chain

2:45–3:05 · 20 sFunctionality

On the slide

  • Two phones’ outboxes flowing into the server, and one chain coming out, with a fork in red.
  • Four cards for the sync guarantees that DSO judges will probe.

“On reconnect, every waiting change goes up. Each has its own ID, so a retry never doubles anything. The server orders events by its own counter, not phone clocks, rebuilds one chain, and names anyone who signed two handovers. And a late return raises an alarm on the phone, offline.”

~50 words
11

Sudden Dev: only enrolled staff get in

3:05–3:30 · 25 sSudden Dev

On the slide

  • Three layers, matching the requirement’s own words: authentication, authorisation, and control.
  • Each layer names the demo moment that will prove it, so the judges know what to watch for.

“Sudden Development asked for authentication and authorisation that work offline. The fingerprint unlocks the same hardware key that signs handovers, checked with no network. Each person’s role and authorisation date are checked on the phone, and again at sync. And the safety officer controls enrolment, a 72-hour lease and remote withdrawal. No login server, yet only Siti can open Siti’s phone.”

~60 words
12

Does it work? Evaluation

3:30–3:55 · 25 sFunctionalityPitching

On the slide

  • The attack table: each threat, what happens today, and what Tenet does.
  • Three numbers as evidence: 110 logic tests (16 sync engine, 13 server rules), 171 screenshot tests, and 3 real phones on 3 Android versions. Recount on the day you pitch.

“Is it effective? We attacked it every way we could: denial, a photographed code, a double handover, a lapsed worker, a stolen phone, a faked clock, a camera never returned. Today these go unseen for weeks. Tenet catches every one, most of them offline and on the spot, backed by over a hundred tests and three real phones.”

~55 words

Why it scores: “evaluation of effectiveness” is an explicit guideline, and most teams skip it. DSO judges think in threat models, so this table speaks their language.

Likely Q&A, one line each

  • Lost phone? Fingerprint lock, 72 h lease, remote withdrawal.
  • Two people collude? They both sign, so both are on record as liable.
  • Real deployment? Supabase can run on the company’s own servers.
  • Why not paper? Paper can’t refuse a double handover at the moment it happens.
  • Lost-phone data? In production: an encrypted database and no screen mirroring (FLAG_SECURE).
