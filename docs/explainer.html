Who holds the source?

An app that lets radiation workers hand over radioactive sources with no network, where both people sign every handover, and the records still add up to one true chain when the phones reconnect.

Part 1

Why "offline" is the hard part

Saving data on a phone with no network is easy. Phones have done it for years. The hard part starts when more than one person records the same thing on different phones. Each phone now holds its own copy, and the copies can disagree.

If everyone could reach one shared database at the moment they act, there would be nothing to sync. The database would simply refuse a second checkout of the same item. Offline work breaks that, because the person acting can't reach the one true record.

The one-book test

Could this process run correctly with one paper book in one place? If yes, it doesn't need a syncing app. A key register at a single cabinet passes the test, so it doesn't need one. Radioactive sources fail it: they leave the store, travel, and change hands far from any book.

Every existing approach gets two of these three properties and gives up the third:

ApproachWorks offlineOne true recordOthers can see it
One paper book in one placeYesYesNo, you have to walk to it
An always-online systemNoYesYes
An offline app that uploads laterYesNo, copies clash silentlyLater
Our appYesYes, every clash caught and proven at syncAt the next sync

Computer scientists know this trade-off as the CAP theorem: while copies are apart, you can't have every property at every instant. Our honest promise is this. While offline, each phone shows how out of date it is. When copies meet, every clash is caught and resolved correctly, and none is silently overwritten.

Why defence cares about this

MINDEF runs classified work on a system "not connected to the Internet". Physical separation is how it contained the 2017 I-net breach. fact

Offline by accident

The regulator, IMDA, said the impact "could have been very serious". reported

DSO's own mission

DSO's National Security Office works to protect critical infrastructure and counter chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) threats. fact

A lost or stolen radioactive source is exactly that kind of threat. Keeping every source accounted for is the prevention side of it.

For a defence engineer, syncing is a controlled crossing between a disconnected place and the system of record. The questions they'll ask are these: who authorised each step, what is logged, and what happens when two records disagree. Our design is built to answer them.

Part 3

The setting: radioactive sources at work

What a sealed source is

A sealed source is a small capsule of radioactive material sealed inside metal. Its radiation passes through steel, so industry uses it like an X-ray. If it's lost, whoever finds and handles it can be seriously injured. If it's stolen, it could be misused in a "dirty bomb". That's why the international rulebook is called the Code of Conduct on the Safety and Security of Radioactive Sources (IAEA), which uses both of the challenge's words.

Where it's used: industrial radiography

To check a weld in a ship hull, a pipeline or a pressure tank, a radiographer takes a radiography camera to the site. The camera is a shielded container with the source inside. The radiographer cordons off the area, places film behind the weld, winds the source out to expose the film, then winds it back. When the job ends, the camera goes back to a secure store.

This happens in Singapore. NEA's licence forms list shipyards and refineries as kinds of business, and industrial radiography as a purpose reported. Handling and transporting sources needs a separate NEA licence (L6A) reported.

What the rules require today

From Singapore's Radiation Protection (Ionising Radiation) Regulations 2023, read from a third-party copy. Verify the regulation numbers on Singapore Statutes Online.

The gap: the law sees a source leave the store and come back, plus one location a day. It doesn't see who holds it in between, and it never asks the receiver to sign.

The pattern: sources go missing while they're out of the store and moving. The loss is found late, and nobody can show who last had it.

One-sided records fail, even in Singapore

Court of Appeal · [2018] SGCA 87

In Mohamed Affandi bin Rosli v Public Prosecutor, two officers gave conflicting accounts of who held drug exhibits between about 6.50 pm and 10.56 pm. The officer who received them didn't record it. The court found "two plausible but irreconcilable versions of the chain of custody" and set the capital convictions aside. fact

Singapore Police Force · 11 Aug 2023

An investigation officer allegedly forged acknowledgement receipts so that his supervisor would believe restitution processes had been followed. It came to light years later, through case reviews. fact

These cases aren't about radioactive sources. They prove the mechanism: a handover that only one person records, or a receipt that only one person controls, can't be trusted later. Requiring both people to sign closes that gap.

Part 6

Our solution: a signed custody token

Every time a radiography camera changes hands, the giver and the receiver both sign the handover on their phones and swap it by QR code, with no network. The latest signed handover shows who is liable now. An overdue return raises an alarm, and when phones reconnect, the server rebuilds one checked chain.

How one handover works

  1. Offer. The giver picks the camera and the receiver from the team list. Their phone signs an offer and shows it as a QR code. The code includes the camera's recent history, so the receiver can check it.
  2. Check, offline. The receiver's phone scans the offer and checks every signature against the team's public keys, which it saved at the last sync. It confirms the giver really is the current holder.
  3. Tie it to the object. The receiver scans the camera's tag or tamper-evident seal. If the number doesn't match, the handover can't go ahead.
  4. Accept. The receiver's phone signs the acceptance and shows it as a QR code. Liability moves the moment the receiver signs.
  5. Confirm. The giver scans the acceptance. Both phones now hold the same record, signed by both, ready to sync.

The chain for one working day

The item

A radiography camera with the serial number of the source inside it. It carries a QR tag and a tamper-evident seal.

Each phone

Holds a private signing key that can't be copied out of the phone, plus every team member's public key, so it can check any signature offline.

The due-back time

Set at sign-out, for example 19:00. Every phone in the chain knows it, so an overdue alarm goes off even with no network.

The server

Rebuilds the chain from whatever phones sync, checks every signature and link, raises fork and overdue alerts, and produces the records the law requires.

Part 8

Why it syncs correctly

We define "correctly" as five properties you can check, plus one more that this setting needs.

1
Nothing is lost.

Each handover is stored on both phones, so either phone can deliver it.

2
Nothing is doubled.

A handover sent twice is the same signed record, so the server keeps one copy.

3
Nothing is silently overwritten.

When two records clash, both are kept and shown to the radiation safety officer.

4
The rules always hold.

One holder at a time, and only authorised workers can accept. A fork or a seal mismatch is never accepted silently.

5
The history is honest.

Every step is signed by both people and linked to the step before it. The phone's time and the server's arrival time are both kept.

+
A missing handover gets noticed.

The overdue alarm means a missing source is noticed at 19:00, not two weeks later.

The design decisions behind it

Proof is a signature

An empty phone proves nothing, because the giver could have hidden the camera. What proves the giver's case is the receiver's signature. It works like signing for a parcel.

Why not an NFT

An NFT relies on a blockchain to record each transfer, and a blockchain needs a network. Offline, an NFT is just a copyable file. A chain that both people sign gives the same guarantee with no network.

Copies get caught

Software can't stop a tampered phone from copying a token while it's offline. But a double handover leaves two records signed by the same giver after the same step. That proves who cheated.

Tied to the object

The receiver scans the seal at every handover. In production, NFC tags that generate a new code on every tap would stop a photo of the tag from being used.

Clocks aren't trusted

The order of events comes from the chain's links and each phone's own counter. A phone's clock is treated as a claim, and the server records when each record actually arrived.

Check by radio

A supervisor reads out a random challenge. The holder's phone turns it into six digits, which the holder reads back. Those digits prove which phone holds the latest chain, without a network or a QR code.

Store officer

Part 10

What we're not claiming

  • We found no lost source in Singapore. Our incidents are from Malaysia, Australia, Thailand and the US, and we say so.
  • We don't know which tools Singapore companies use today. We only know what the law requires.
  • Not every site is offline. Handovers often happen where signal is poor or phones are restricted, and a handover signed by both people is useful even online (Part 4).
  • The chain is tamper-evident, not tamper-proof. A tampered phone can misbehave, but it can't forge another person's signature.
  • It can't stop a capsule falling off a truck. What it can do is raise the alarm on time, and name the last person who signed for it.
  • The demo uses dummy items only, and we describe source security only at the level of public rules.
