## Who the safety personnel are (official titles)

- **Licensee.** The company or institution holding the NEA licence. It carries the legal duties. NEA administers the Radiation Protection Act 2007 and its regulations through licensing, notification, authorisation, inspection and enforcement. [fact] NEA, https://www.nea.gov.sg/our-services/radiation-safety/regulatory-information/summary-of-radiation-protection-act-2007 (via search result, accessed 6 Nov 2026) [search summary only]
- **Radiation Safety Officer.** The 2023 regulations use "radiation safety officer", not "radiation protection officer". Reg 19(1): a licensee must appoint one if the Director-General (NEA) requires it. The person needs suitable academic knowledge or professional experience and technical competency in radiation protection. NEA must approve the appointment (reg 19(3)-(4)) and may revoke it (reg 19(5)). Reg 19(6): when the licensee is absent, the officer is responsible for radiation safety in work under their supervision. [reported] Mirror of SSO text, Radiation Protection (Ionising Radiation) Regulations 2023 (S 85/2023), https://littdb.sfo2.cdn.digitaloceanspaces.com/litt/SG/SSOStatutes/subsidiary_legislation/RPA2007-S85-2023.html (accessed 6 Nov 2026). Verify on sso.agc.gov.sg.
- **"Suitably qualified persons".** NEA's own summary says licensees designate these to carry out radiation protection duties. It does not define an RSO or RPO title. [fact] NEA, https://www.nea.gov.sg/our-services/radiation-safety/regulatory-information/summary-of-ionising-radiation-regulations-2023 (accessed 6 Nov 2026)
- **Radiation worker.** Must be registered with NEA. Registration needs a medical examination within the 12 months before applying. Nobody under 18 may do radiation work. [fact] Same NEA summary.
- **Educational institutions.** A "competent employee or staff" designated by the principal or head takes responsibility for sealed sources. [fact] Same NEA summary.
- **"Radiation Protection Officer" as a statutory Singapore title.** Not found.
- **Safe for the pitch:** say "Radiation Safety Officer and registered radiation workers", and note that the RSO duty applies where NEA requires it.

## The security process, step by step, with its legal basis

All regulation numbers below come from the S 85/2023 mirror [reported] unless marked [fact] (NEA summary).

1. **Licence.** Import, export, sale, possession and use of radioactive materials need an NEA licence. Licence names seen in search results: IR2 (keep, possess for use or use), L4, L6A (handle and transport). [search summary only] https://www.gobusiness.gov.sg/browse-all-licences/National-Environment-Agency-(NEA)/IR2---Licence-to-keep--possess-for-use-(other-than-sale)-or-use-irradiating-apparatus-or-radioactive-materials
2. **Storage and security.** Licensees must keep security measures that prevent unauthorised access to radioactive material in use, storage or transport, and NEA may require improvements. [fact] NEA summary. Reg 25 covers safe containment (for example storage containers for quantities over 35 GBq). Part 16 (security) was past the point I could read, so its text is not read.
3. **Records (reg 26(1)).** The person must keep a record of: date of receipt, quantity, nature and form, activity, and location (updated each working day). For sealed sources it must also hold the identifying number, movement history and disposal. [reported] The NEA summary agrees on receipt, quantity, form, activity and location "at any time". [fact]
4. **Annual checks.** Sealed-source inventories are reviewed at least once a year to find disused sources. A wipe (leak) test is done on each sealed source every 12 months. [fact] NEA summary.
5. **Moving a source inside licensed premises (reg 27).** It must be in a suitable container, carried by an authorised radiation worker, and the recipient must know it is a sealed source. [reported] This is the nearest thing in law to a handover rule. It asks for no signature or record of the handover itself. [inference]
6. **Supply to another party (reg 24).** Sales and supplies go only to authorised persons or licence applicants. The supplier must make reasonable efforts to confirm the licence follows, must "promptly notify" NEA if it won't, and must keep records of all supplies (reg 24(5)(b)). [reported] NEA summary agrees. [fact]
7. **Transport.** NEA's summary says transport of radioactive materials is covered by separate regulations and does not describe them. [fact] Transport rules: not found.
8. **Loss reporting.**
   - An employee who believes material is lost or missing must "immediately notify the licensee" (reg 26(3)). The licensee must "immediately notify the Director-General" and take immediate steps to find it (reg 26(4)). [reported]
   - NEA's summary lists "lost, stolen or attempted theft" as notify NEA "immediately". [fact]
   - The hour deadlines (oral report within 24 hours, written within 48 hours, full report within 10 days) are given for **a security breach and a radiation accident**, not for a lost source. [fact] NEA summary. The regulation text I read gave no hour or day deadline for loss, only "immediately", and does not name theft in the reporting provisions. Treat "immediately" as the loss deadline.
9. **Disused sources.** Return to the supplier first. [fact] NEA summary. Singapore also told the IAEA in September 2022 that it commits politically to the IAEA guidance on import and export of sources and on disused sources. [search summary only] https://www.iaea.org/sites/default/files/documents/review-missions/irrs_report.pdf (IRRS 2022 report)

**International framing.**
- The IAEA publication is titled "Code of Conduct on the Safety and Security of Radioactive Sources" (2004), so the word "security" is in the title. [search summary only] https://www-pub.iaea.org/mtcd/publications/pdf/code-2004_web.pdf
- The Code says each State should keep a national register of sources, covering at least Category 1 and 2 sources. [search summary only] (same search, IAEA pages)
- IAEA security guidance for radioactive material: "Nuclear Security Recommendations on Radioactive Material and Associated Facilities", https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/Pub1487_web.pdf [search summary only, content not read]. Categorisation of sources: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub1227_web.pdf [search summary only].
- Not sourced, from memory, verify before use: industrial radiography sources are generally in IAEA Category 2.
- Whether Singapore has adopted the Code itself: not found. Only the 2022 commitment to the two supplementary guidance documents is seen.

## The current solution and its gaps

- **Paper or in-house logs under reg 26.** What Singapore licensees actually use: not found. That they are mostly paper or in-house spreadsheets is [inference].
- **US National Source Tracking System (NSTS).** For Category 1 and 2 sources, every manufacture, transfer, receipt, disassembly or disposal must be reported by the close of the next business day. Both the shipper and the receiver must report, and if the receipt report does not arrive the system alerts regulators. Reports go online, in batch files, or by fax, mail or phone. [search summary only] https://www.nrc.gov/security/byproduct/ismp/nsts/nsts-rule.html ; https://www.nrc.gov/security/byproduct/ismp/nsts/faqs
  - This is the closest existing system to our two-signature idea. [inference]
  - Gap [inference]: it is a licensee-to-licensee ledger with a next-business-day lag. It does not record person-to-person handovers inside a licensee (store keeper to radiographer to crew to vehicle to site), and it assumes a network or a fax.
- **GPS on trucks and cameras.** The NRC's Part 37 rule (March 2013) did not require GPS, calling it "neither justified nor necessary". 8 of 15 radiography companies visited by GAO had installed it voluntarily. A 2011 Sandia study found existing technologies such as GPS had limitations that prevented reliable tracking. [fact] GAO-14-293, https://gao.gov/assets/670/664075.txt
- **Coverage.** A state regulator told the NRC that cell coverage is spotty in many areas, so crews in remote areas often cannot reach their home office. PNNL says attaching tracking to mobile radiography sources is hard because of size, storage and shipping container limits, and describes its Mobile Source Transit Security system with an internal radiation detector. [search summary only] https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML0813/ML081370660.pdf ; https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/security-mobile-radiological-sources-overview-industry-applied-technologies
- **Viet Nam** runs a Radioactive Source Location Tracking System, based on a Korean system, linking field workers to the regulator. [search summary only] https://www.iaea.org/sites/default/files/publications/magazines/bulletin/bull57-4/5741617.pdf
- **Vendor software.** Not found.
- **Gap summary [inference]:** location trackers answer "where is the truck?" They do not answer "which named person holds the source at this minute, and did they sign for it?" They also fail where there is no signal, or when the source leaves the vehicle.

## Where handovers happen, and why they're offline

Handover points implied by the law and the incidents [inference unless marked]:
- store keeper to radiographer at the start of a job, and back at the end (the 2012 US case: camera left in the truck instead of being returned to storage [fact, GAO])
- radiographer to assistant or second crew member
- crew to driver or transport firm (the Mexico and WA cases were both in a transport chain)
- vehicle to site and site to RSO
- licensee to another licensee (supply, disused-source return), where reg 24 requires supplier records

Why offline [inference unless marked]:
- Documented, US: crews in remote areas often cannot reach the office by cell phone. [search summary only]
- Jobs inside ship hulls, double bottoms, tanks, pressure vessels and reactor towers are likely to be out of signal. Offshore rigs and vessels at sea have limited or costly links. Radiography is often at night inside exclusion zones. All of this needs checking.
- Petrochemical sites such as Jurong Island may restrict phones in some zones. Not found, to verify.
- Whether shipyards, Jurong Island or offshore sites have network coverage: **not found**. Singapore's general coverage is strong, so the offline argument is weaker here than in Australia or Malaysia. The honest pitch is "offline when it matters", not "Singapore has no signal".

## How our concept applies (the handover chain, the items, the seals)

- **Items.** Each sealed source has an identifying number that reg 26(1) already requires in the records. That is the natural item ID for the QR tag. Candidates: radiography cameras (iridium-192, as in the Malaysian and US cases), portable and fixed gauge sources, and check sources. [inference]
- **People.** Only authorised, NEA-registered radiation workers may carry a sealed source inside premises (reg 27). That maps to who is allowed to sign a handover in the app. [inference from reg 27]
- **Handover.** Giver and receiver both sign on their phones and swap the signed record by QR with no network. The receiver also scans the tag or seal on the container. The result is the signed, offline-checkable chain that reg 26's "movement history" and "location updated each working day" ask for in spirit. [inference]
- **Seals.** Tamper-evident seals on storage and transport containers: scanning the seal serial at each handover records "seal intact, number matches". The app can only check the serial number, not whether a physical seal is genuine. [inference]
- **Sync.** The server rebuilds the chain and flags a double handover (the same custodian signing the same item to two people) or a gap (a handover given but never received). The NSTS already uses the "shipped but not received" alert idea at licensee level. [search summary only for NSTS; the application is inference]
- **After a loss.** The last valid signed handover narrows the search from "somewhere between A and B over up to two weeks" (WA) to a named person, place and time. [inference]

## One demo moment

"Two people, one source." Two phones, no Wi-Fi. A store keeper hands a radiography camera to a radiographer: both sign, QR swapped, the radiographer scans the seal and it shows green. Then the same store keeper, still offline, signs the same camera over to a second crew. Later both phones come online and the server marks that item red: "Double handover at 14:02: one custodian, two receivers." The screen shows the chain and which signature is the first valid one. Second beat: tap "report lost" and the screen shows the last valid custodian, place and time, so the search area shrinks from days to one leg. Use a fictional source, fictional site and fake serial numbers.

## Weak spots and sensitivity

- **No Singapore incident found.** The "it has gone wrong" evidence is regional or foreign. Say so up front.
- **Most incidents were vehicle losses or hijacks** (Malaysia 2018, WA, Mexico, US thefts), not a person handing a source to another person. A digital chain does not stop a capsule falling off a truck. It names the last custodian and can raise an "unreceived handover" alarm. Do not claim it would have prevented those losses.
- **Offline is weaker in Singapore** than in the Australian and Malaysian examples. Needs a defensible reason (hulls and tanks, offshore, restricted zones). Unverified.
- **Few users and few handovers a day.** A radiography crew might do a handful. Judges may ask why not just use paper and a phone call. Answer: signed, tamper-evident, checkable by a stranger offline.
- **Seal authenticity.** A phone reads a serial number or QR, which can be photographed. A pitch that claims it "proves" the seal is intact overclaims.
- **Real regulator duties.** NEA wants "immediately" notification and its own register; the app must not appear to replace those. Frame it as the licensee's internal custody record that feeds the legal record. [inference]
- **Sensitivity.** Radioactive source locations and security gaps are security information. Use fake data only. Do not describe how to find or steal a source. Avoid dirty-bomb scare slides: keep the framing on accountability and safety, and keep Thailand's incident details to the official facts (no contamination found in metal products). Do not name individuals or companies beyond what the sources state, and attribute any company mention to the press source.
